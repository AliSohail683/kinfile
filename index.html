The 30-day access checklist

The steps Kinfile will walk you through, in plain English. Do them while your parent can still take part and sign.

  1. Get the core documents drafted.A durable financial power of attorney and a healthcare proxy, ideally from an elder-law attorney in your parent's state. Ask for wording that covers digital accounts.
  2. Register the POA with each bank and brokerage, before you need it.Many institutions review a POA themselves or ask for their own form, and it's rarely done over the phone. Get written confirmation that it was accepted.
  3. Become a trusted contact at brokerages.Brokerages must ask customers for a trusted contact. It doesn't give you control of the account, but it lets the firm call you if they suspect fraud or can't reach your parent.
  4. Ask about view-only or caregiver access.Some banks now let a caregiver see activity with their own login, without sharing your parent's password.
  5. Fix the contact details on file.With your parent's OK, make sure verification codes and letters go to a phone number and address someone in the family actually controls.
  6. Turn on alerts.Large withdrawals, new payees and missed payments are the early signs of scams and slipping memory.
  7. Social Security: ask about a representative payee.Social Security doesn't act on a power of attorney. If your parent needs help managing benefits, it appoints a representative payee instead.
  8. Medicare: get authorized to talk to them.Your parent can name you by calling 1-800-MEDICARE or by filing Form CMS-10106, so Medicare can discuss their claims with you.
  9. IRS: file Form 2848 or 8821.Form 2848 lets an immediate family member represent your parent. Form 8821 just lets you see their tax information.
  10. Set up the phone for emergencies.Turn on Medical ID with "Show When Locked", add a Legacy Contact (Apple) or Inactive Account Manager (Google), and consider Assistive Access for a simpler home screen.
  11. Map the accounts from the mail.For a month or two, note every statement, bill and renewal that arrives. That's your parent's real account list.
  12. Write down where things are.The will, deeds, insurance policies, the safe-deposit box key, and how to get into the phone and email.

This checklist is general information, not legal or financial advice. Rules vary by state and by institution, so confirm the details with each one and with an attorney.

Questions

Good to know

Is Kinfile a password manager?

No. Passwords belong in a real password manager like Apple Passwords, Bitwarden or 1Password, and Kinfile works alongside them. Kinfile tracks the legal and practical access you need at each institution, and notes where each login lives.

Will you ask for my parent's bank logins?

No. Sharing a bank login can weaken fraud protections and isn't how banks want families to help. Kinfile points you to the official routes: power of attorney, trusted contacts, caregiver access and alerts.

What if my parent doesn't want help yet?

Kinfile is built around consent. Your parent can see what's shared and with whom. Many families start by framing it as "in case something happens", not as planning for decline.

Is this legal advice?

No. Kinfile organizes the work and explains the usual steps. For the documents themselves, talk to an elder-law attorney in your parent's state.
